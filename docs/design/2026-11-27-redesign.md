# Redesign of the robot: requirements, body targets, tiers and control

Date: 2026-11-27. Issue: #58. Decided by the maintainer; this record was written with an AI assistant from the
maintainer's restated requirements and a literature survey (kept as internal notes). Prices are public listings seen
between 2026-11-24 and 2026-11-27 (about ¥150 per US dollar), to be checked again before ordering (#60); values marked
*(unverified)* come from search summaries, not from the source itself.

## Why

The first design walked with a hand-made trot gait in MuJoCo, but the learned policies were jittery and crouched. The
body is tall and narrow, slightly tail-heavy, and its knees work near the servo limit while trotting (baseline below).
The survey adds: the knee servo at the end of the thigh makes the legs heavy and slow; the servos cannot absorb
impacts; and the learned policies lacked smoothness constraints, a body-height term, a servo model with delay, and a
foot-placement rule in the base gait.

## Requirements (restated by the maintainer, 2026-11-27)

| # | Requirement |
|---|---|
| R1 | Small and compact body; low centre of mass; balanced front–back and left–right; body targets checked in MuJoCo before ordering |
| R2 | Motions, in priority order: (1) a learned policy (PPO) walks stably on the robot; (2) sharp tracking of speed and heading commands; (3) easy training on CPUs and small inference on the microcontrollers; (4) standing up after a fall. Acrobatic motion (jumps, flips) is not a requirement and stays outside erg (#27) |
| R3 | Computation: the joint policy and state estimation run on the per-leg ESP32s, which exchange values with neighbouring legs only; training runs on a PC's CPU in simulation, not on the robot. No vision and no high-level decisions; a head can later carry a computer |
| R4 | Sensors: no camera. Joint angles and loads (serial bus servos), IMU, foot contact, range sensors if needed. A head part may hold sensors and a computer |
| R5 | Compliance: mounting points for rubber or springs in the feet and legs, for stability and as an entry point for findings of [poise](https://github.com/physical-edge-native/poise) later |
| R6 | Per-leg control and one whole-body policy compared on the same body, sensors and training budget (scope record); the Dyne parts used and the effort to connect them are recorded |
| R7 | When learning fails, the causes are separated: body design versus training setup (reward, observation, simulation-to-reality gap) |

## Decided (2026-11-27)

| Ordering | The parts list is revised (#60) before anything is ordered | servo voltage changes the battery and the charger |
| Knee servo (2026-11-27, #59) | In the thigh, just below the hip flexion shaft, driving the shank through a parallelogram push rod | keeping it on the shoulder coaxial with the hip servo made the body about 290 mm wide; in the thigh it is about 240 mm wide, the knee servo's angle is still the knee angle, and the leg's inertia about the hip rises by about a tenth |
| Leg target (2026-11-27, #59) | The leg's moment of inertia about the hip flexion shaft replaces the leg-mass fraction | the fraction stood for leg inertia; with the knee servo in the thigh near the shaft the fraction is 0.34 while the inertia stays low |

## Body targets checked in MuJoCo

`erg/body_targets.py` computes them from the model; `scripts/body_targets.py` prints them next to the targets;
`tests/test_body_targets.py` checks the baseline below. The new body must meet the targets (#59).

| Quantity | Target | Baseline: the current design | Reason for the target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total mass | ≤ 1.2 kg | 1.40 kg | smaller loads; Cheetah-Cub ran at 1.1 kg with similar servos |
| Centre-of-mass height ÷ half of the support width (standing) | ≤ 1.5 (static sideways tipping at ≥ 34°) | 3.40 (0.170 m over 0.100 m; 16.4°) | a trot is supported on a diagonal line, so the sideways margin matters most |
| Centre-of-mass offset from the support centre | ≤ 5 mm each way | 12.5 mm backwards | |
| Leg's moment of inertia about the hip flexion shaft (one leg, standing) | ≤ 3.8 × 10⁻⁴ kg m² (since #59; the leg-mass fraction ≤ 0.20 before) | leg-mass fraction 0.38 | lighter, faster legs, as in Cheetah-Cub, Mini Cheetah, Doggo and Pupper; 3.8 × 10⁻⁴ is 1.2 times the layout with the knee servo on the shoulder |
| Joint torque ÷ servo stall torque in the hand-made trot at 0.2 m/s, worst joint's 95th percentile | ≤ 0.5 | 0.86 | margin for pushes and steps |
| Same, maximum | ≤ 0.8 | 0.97 | |
| Joint speed asked of the servos ÷ no-load speed in the same trot (change of the joint targets per control period), worst joint's 95th percentile | ≤ 0.6 | 0.71 (measured joint velocity) | servos lose torque near their no-load speed. Since #59 the commanded speed is used: the measured velocity also holds the passive vibration after each touch-down, which the servo does not drive |
| Joint torque ÷ stall torque while standing up from a crouch in 2 s, maximum | ≤ 0.9 | 0.32 | |

The baseline uses the 7.4 V STS3215 (1.91 N·m, 5.5 rad/s); the new body is checked with the chosen servo's values.
Rolling over from the side is added with the new body (#59), because the current abduction range (±0.5 rad) cannot
roll the body.

## Body L1 as designed (#59)

The numbers live in `erg/design.py`; `scripts/make_model.py` writes `models/quadruped.xml` from them, and
`erg/cad_l1.py` draws the parts at layout level (where each part is and how much space it takes; screw holes, pins
and the push rod's ends come after a test print of one leg). Printed masses in the model are the parts'.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Servo (12 V class; seller's values, to be checked on a real servo) | 2.94 N·m stall, 4.72 rad/s no load, 55 g (taken as the STS3215's) |
| Abduction shafts | x ±85 mm, y ±40 mm; abduction range ±2.6 rad (to get up from the back) |
| Legs | 60 mm outward of the abduction shafts; thigh 95 mm, shank 100 mm; standing foot 140 mm below and 8 mm behind the hip flexion shaft |
| Hand-made trot | period 0.45 s (0.35 s before), step height 12 mm (15 mm before), within the servo's speed |
| Total mass | 1.199 kg: servos 0.66 kg, printed parts 0.30 kg, battery 0.12 kg (estimate), electronics 0.06 kg (estimate), wiring 0.04 kg (estimate) |
| Body targets | all met (`scripts/body_targets.py`): centre-of-mass height ÷ half support width 1.40 (tipping at 35°), offset under 2 mm, leg inertia 3.7 × 10⁻⁴ kg m², trot torque 0.42 / 0.48, commanded speed 0.57, standing up from a crouch 0.21 |
| Standing up after a fall | a fixed sequence (`erg/righting.py`) found by a random search in MuJoCo with the legs colliding with the torso; it gets up from the back, either side and standing |

The mass is at the limit of its target; heavier servos or battery than estimated would exceed it, and are checked
when the parts arrive.

The hand-made trot is open loop and with this servo model covers about 0.4 of the commanded speed (0.65–0.87 with the
first body): the model's servo lags more. Following the commanded speed is the job of the base controller's foot
placement and of the learned policy.

## Body layout options

| Option | Layout | For | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| **L1 (chosen)** | All three servos of a leg at the shoulder; the knee driven by a push rod (as in Stanford Pupper); feet spread outward; body lower; flat torso with the battery at the bottom centre; head mount at the front; abduction range wide enough to roll over | low leg inertia, wide support, low centre of mass; simple serial-leg kinematics for the base gait | the push rod adds parts and some play |
| L2 | Keep the serial leg (knee servo at the thigh end); widen and lower the body | least redesign | heavy legs remain; knee torque stays the limit |
| L3 | Five-bar parallel leg | symmetric, low inertia | new kinematics, less range under the body, most redesign |

Compliance (R5): replaceable soft foot caps, and a spring mount (a pin and a slot) that can stay rigid at first.

## Performance tiers

What limits each motion, from the survey:

- Walking and command tracking need torque margin at the knee and hip, light legs, and a base controller with a
  foot-placement rule. Hobby servos suffice on a light, low body (Cheetah-Cub: 1.1 kg, 2 N·m hobby servos, 1.42 m/s).
- Push recovery and steps need margin and quick foot placement; the servo's delay is the main risk (one low-cost
  quadruped measured 76 ms), so the servos are identified in any case.
- Standing up after a fall is slow and torque-bound; small servo robots do it (Petoi Bittle, about 0.3 kg).
- Jumping is limited by speed and power, not torque: the eight hip and knee servos give about 15 W/kg against about
  70 W/kg for a 20 cm jump (estimate); robots that jump use low-gear brushless actuators. It is not a requirement.

| Tier | Motions | Actuators | Parts cost (estimate) |
|---|---|---|---|
| T1 minimum | a learned policy walks stably and follows speed and heading | 12 serial bus servos with angle and load feedback (12 V class, about 2.9 N·m, about $17–22 *(unverified)*; or the 7.4 V STS3215, 1.9 N·m, ¥3,245) | about ¥97,000–110,000 |
| T2 target (chosen) | T1 + recovers from pushes, tolerates steps and slopes, tracks commands sharply, stands up after a fall | the same servos; foot force sensors; optional range-sensor head | about ¥105,000–125,000 |

Parts common to both tiers: four ESP32-S3 boards, four IMUs, foot switches, 5 V regulators (input up to 36 V), servo
bus adapters, bench power supply, small parts, PLA. The battery and charger follow the servo voltage (3S for the 12 V
class). The revised list with checked prices is made in #60.

## Control architecture (the same for both forms)

1. Base controller on each leg's ESP32, from its own sensors and its neighbours' values: a phase oscillator
   (amplitude and phase separately); foot placement by the capture-point rule (Raibert's rule with gain about 1/ω ≈
   0.1 s, forward and sideways); body attitude by leg-length differences from the IMU; swing-leg retraction; stumble
   and early or late touch-down reflexes. Neighbouring legs couple in anti-phase, which makes the trot a stable
   solution on the ring of neighbours.
2. A learned policy at about 25 Hz adjusts the base controller (frequency, foot-placement residuals of about
   ±0.02 m, step height, gains). Per-leg: one shared network per leg; central: one network for all legs; the same
   outputs per leg.
3. Smoothness and posture: a Lipschitz-constrained policy (gradient penalty) or CAPS; penalties on first and second
   differences of the actions; body-height and posture terms; small residuals.
4. Servo model: published identifications of the STS3215 (BAM; Open Duck Mini v2) as a starting point, then erg's
   own servos identified on a bench; delay randomised and measured separately for the per-leg and central wiring.
5. Training budget matched in environment steps; ten seeds per form for the main comparison.
6. Inference unchanged: Dyne's mlp-actor on the ESP32-S3, bit-exact with the PC.

These are worked out in later issues; the training design record (2026-11-26) stays valid for the parts it covers.

## Separating causes when learning fails (R7)

In order, each with a pass condition written before it is run, in simulation and then on the robot:

1. Body: the hand-made trot and the base controller without learning (speeds, falls, the body targets above).
2. Simulation gap: the same joint commands replayed on the robot and in MuJoCo; joint angles and body motion compared.
3. Learning: the policy on the base controller with the identified servo model; reward terms, observations and
   smoothness changed one at a time on a fixed set of seeds.

## Training in a room measured by Dyne

Policies are also trained and evaluated in a room measured by Dyne, as a place only: the policy does not observe the
room (#61). Dyne adds an MJCF export next to its USD export; whether that export becomes a listed contract is proposed
in physical-edge-native/pena-meta#15.

## Options not chosen

- Tier T3 with low-gear brushless actuators for jumps (about ¥170,000–300,000, rough): dropped with acrobatic motion.
- The 7.4 V STS3215: cheaper by little, with less torque margin than the baseline already lacks.
