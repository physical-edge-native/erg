# Servo model with delay and torque-speed limit (Issue #70)

Status: implemented in simulation (`erg/servo.py`), with the bench script and the fit (`scripts/servo_bench.py`,
#88); every parameter is unverified until the bench identification below is done on the real servo. Written with
the help of an AI assistant.

## Why

The redesign record (`docs/design/2026-11-27-redesign.md`, control architecture item 4) asks for a servo model with
delay before training: the learned policies of #51 were trained with MuJoCo's ideal position actuator, which acts on
a command in the same 2 ms step and has no gain or delay to randomise. The servos are 12 V class serial bus servos
(Waveshare ST3215), not yet bought or measured; published identifications of the similar 7.4 V Feetech STS3215 are the
starting point.

## The model

For each of the 12 joints, before every simulation step of 2 ms:

1. **Delay.** The commanded joint position enters the servo's control loop `delay` seconds after it was sent,
   rounded to whole simulation steps (a ring buffer). A delay is any number of control periods (20 ms) plus a
   fraction, in 2 ms steps.
2. **Position loop.** Drive torque u = kp (target − q) − kd q̇.
3. **Drive limit.** u is clipped to ± the stall torque: the motor's voltage saturates.
4. **Back electromotive force.** The motor's torque falls by b q̇ with b = stall torque / no-load speed. MuJoCo applies
   it as the joint's damping, which its implicit integrator handles stably.
5. **Friction and rotor inertia.** Coulomb friction (MuJoCo `frictionloss`) and the reflected rotor inertia
   (`armature`) on the joint.

The torque on the joint is τ = clip(u, ±τ_stall) − b q̇. In the direction of motion it is at most
τ_stall (1 − |q̇| / ω₀): the limit falls linearly from the stall torque at rest to zero at the no-load speed ω₀, the
same in both directions. Against the motion (braking) it may reach τ_stall (1 + |q̇| / ω₀), as for a voltage-limited
DC motor (the model used by BAM, below). Clipping the braking torque to the same falling line was not chosen: a leg
driven past the no-load speed by the ground or gravity would then get no torque at all.

This is the structure the model's position actuator already had (`kp` 10 N·m/rad, force limit ± stall torque, joint
damping stall / no-load speed); the servo model adds the delay, the derivative gain, the identified values and
randomisation. The model's file (`models/quadruped.xml`, generated by `erg/design.py`) is not changed: `erg.servo.Servo`
turns the loaded model's position actuators into torque actuators in memory and writes u into `data.ctrl` each step,
so the joint load sensors read u as before.

Use: `erg.env.Env(servo=ServoParams(), randomize_servo=True)`; `erg.body_targets.compute(servo=ServoParams())`;
`uv run python scripts/body_targets.py --servo`. Without these options nothing changes.

## Pass criteria (`tests/test_servo.py`)

- With no delay and the position actuator's gain, friction and limits (`ServoParams.position_actuator()`), the joint
  positions over 2 s of the hand-made trot equal those of the position actuator within 1e-6 rad. On Linux they are
  bit for bit equal (same operations in the same order); the tolerance allows a compiler that fuses multiply and add
  (macOS on ARM), whose differences of about 1e-16 grow through the foot contacts. 1e-6 rad is over a thousand times
  finer than the servo's position resolution (2π / 4096 ≈ 1.5e-3 rad). The same with limits of 1e6 N·m against the
  position actuator without its force limit.
- A target step sent with a delay of n simulation steps (10 and 30 ms) leaves the simulation bit for bit unchanged for
  n steps and changes it in the next.
- With a gain of 200 N·m/rad that saturates the drive, the joint torque never leaves the window above, and in the
  direction of motion never exceeds τ_stall (1 − |q̇| / ω₀).
- The randomised servo is deterministic for a seed; the drawn delay stays in its range.
- `tests/test_body_targets.py`: with `position_actuator()` the body targets equal the model's bit for bit; with the
  nominal servo they are finite and deterministic. `tests/test_gait.py`: the hand-made trot with the nominal servo
  does not fall and covers 0.3–1.2 of the commanded speed at 0.1 and 0.23 m/s.

## Body targets with the servo model

`uv run python scripts/body_targets.py --servo` (nominal parameters), next to the model's position actuator:

| Quantity | Target | Position actuator | Servo model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Centre-of-mass height ÷ half support width | ≤ 1.5 | 1.41 | 1.42 |
| Centre-of-mass offset (x) | ≤ 5 mm | 2.5 mm | 2.7 mm |
| Trot torque ÷ stall, worst joint's 95th percentile | ≤ 0.5 | 0.41 | **0.68 (misses)** |
| Trot torque ÷ stall, maximum | ≤ 0.8 | 0.47 | 0.73 |
| Commanded joint speed ÷ no-load speed | ≤ 0.6 | 0.57 | 0.57 (from the targets; unchanged) |
| Standing up from a crouch, torque ÷ stall | ≤ 0.9 | 0.20 | 0.23 |

Mass and leg inertia do not depend on the actuator. The trot's torque target is missed with the identified values.
One parameter at a time on top of the position actuator (worst joint's 95th percentile): kp 13.37 → 0.46; armature
0.027 with friction 0.068 → 0.53; delay 10 ms → 0.48 (20–40 ms → 0.41); all together 0.68 (0.58 without delay). The
largest single cause is the armature, the rotor's inertia seen at the joint, which the leg has to accelerate at
every step. The hand-made trot with the servo model reaches the stall torque at 0.23 m/s (0.60 of it at 0.1 m/s) and
covers 0.64–0.82 of the commanded speed (about 0.55 with the position actuator).

Whether this changes the body or the gait is a decision for the maintainer; the armature and gain of the 12 V servo
are measured first (below), since they are the values that decide it.

## Bench identification plan for the 12 V servo

Rig: one servo on a fixed stand, driving an arm with a known mass at a known length (a pendulum; two or three
loads, plus no load); 12 V bench supply with current reading; the servo bus adapter on the leg's ESP32-S3 (the same
firmware path as on the robot) or a USB bus adapter on the PC. Time: about half a day for one servo, then a shorter
check of two more servos for spread.

Logged on the serial bus at the highest rate the bus allows (target ≥ 200 Hz, one servo addressed): commanded
position with a host time stamp, present position, present speed, present load, voltage, current and temperature,
each with the time it was read. The bus round trip time is logged separately (a read of one register in a loop).

Experiments:

1. **Delay.** Small steps (0.05 rad) from rest: the time from the bus write to the first change of present position,
   minus half the read round trip; repeated 50 times. Also with the per-leg wiring (three servos on one bus) and the
   central wiring (twelve servos on one bus) as decided in the redesign record, because the delay differs.
2. **Steps.** Steps of 0.1, 0.3, 0.6 and 1.0 rad up and down, each load: rise time, overshoot, final error. Large
   steps saturate the drive and give the stall torque (initial acceleration × inertia) and the no-load speed (the
   speed reached with no load).
3. **Chirp.** Sinusoidal sweep 0.5–10 Hz, amplitudes 0.05 and 0.2 rad, each load: the frequency response.
4. **Friction.** Slow ramps (0.1 rad/s) both ways with no load and the torque read from present load; the static and
   Coulomb friction from the load at which motion starts and its level while moving.
5. **Firmware gains.** The P, D and I registers read back and logged, so that kp and kd can be related to them.

Fitted (least squares on simulated against measured trajectories of a one-joint MuJoCo model of the rig, as BAM does;
BAM itself can be used if its models fit): delay, kp, kd, stall torque, back electromotive force b (or no-load
speed), Coulomb friction, armature. The load reading is calibrated against the known pendulum torque, since the
environment's observation uses it. Result: a new `ServoParams` with the measured values and sources, the randomisation
ranges set from the spread over servos and runs, and the body targets of this record recomputed.

## Bench script and model fit (Issue #88)

`scripts/servo_bench.py` carries out the plan above from a PC; `erg/bench.py` holds the code and `erg/sts.py` the
serial-bus packets.

- **Packets.** The same packets as the leg firmware's `sts.c`; `tests/test_sts.py` checks them byte for byte against
  the firmware's C code compiled for the PC. Goals are sent as the robot sends them (a sync write, no reply); the
  bench also reads the block from present position to present current (15 bytes) and writes torque enable.
- **Run.** `run` pings the servo, reads its P, D and I gains, enables the torque at the present position, brings the
  arm to the hanging position in the first second, then runs the schedule: `full` is 50 small steps for the delay,
  steps of 0.1–1.0 rad both ways (twice), chirps 0.5–10 Hz at 0.05 and 0.2 rad, and slow ramps both ways (about 2.5
  minutes); `short` is a few seconds of the same kinds. Each loop of 5 ms writes the goal and reads the block; the
  log (CSV) holds, per loop, the goal's write time and angle, the reading's time (when the request was sent), position,
  speed, load, voltage, temperature, current, error bits, the raw register values and the experiment's name; the
  rig (arm mass and length, load mass) is in the header. Targets are clipped to ±1.2 rad; the run stops and switches
  the torque off above 65 °C, below 10 V, above 3 A, or on any bus error.
- **Fit.** `fit` simulates the rig (`Rig`: an arm of uniform mass with a load at its end, hanging down at zero) in
  MuJoCo with erg.servo's model, applies the logged goals at their logged times, and minimises the squared
  differences of position (weight 1 / 2e-3 rad) and speed (weight 1 / 0.2 rad/s) at the logged reading times. The
  delay is searched in whole simulation steps (2 ms, the resolution of the robot's model); kp, kd, friction,
  armature, stall torque and no-load speed by Levenberg-Marquardt with finite differences, starting from the
  nominal values. It also reports the ratio of the logged load to the simulated drive torque, the load reading's
  calibration. Several logs (different loads) can be fitted together.
- **Dry run.** `dry-run` does the same against `SimServoPort`, a simulated servo on a simulated bus (each byte takes
  its time at 1 Mbaud; the servo model runs on the rig up to the bus's clock; the position is read in whole steps),
  with known parameters that differ from the starting values, and checks the fit (`tests/test_servo_bench.py`, about
  6 s). Tolerances (`erg.bench.FIT_TOLERANCE`): delay exact; kp, armature, stall torque and no-load speed within 8 %;
  friction within 10 %; kd within 0.05 N·m·s/rad. Grounds: five trials with random parameters (±25 % of the nominal
  values, delays 4–20 ms) recovered the delay exactly, kp, armature, stall torque and no-load speed within 4 %
  and friction within 3 %, with the default rig (0.3 kg at 0.15 m). With a light load (0.1 kg at 0.10 m), whose
  inertia is a twentieth of the rotor's, kp and armature drifted together by up to 12 %: the load must be heavy
  enough. kd came out between 0 and its true value (≤ 0.042): in motion that does not saturate the drive it only
  adds to the back electromotive force's damping, so a short log hardly tells them apart; the full schedule's fast
  chirps are meant to.

The simulated servo uses the fit's own model, so the dry run checks the code path and the fit's ability to recover
parameters, not that the model fits a real servo; that is what the bench run (#87) shows, through the remaining
error (rms position and speed) of the fit.

### Operator's guide (for the bench run, Issue #87)

Requires: one servo, a 12 V bench supply with an adjustable current limit, the servo bus adapter (a USB serial bus
adapter for Feetech servos), a stand that holds the servo with its shaft horizontal, a printed arm with a load at a
measured length, a kitchen scale (to 1 g) and a ruler. Install pyserial: `uv sync --extra bench`.

1. **Measure the rig.** Weigh the arm without the load and the load; measure the shaft-to-load-centre length. These
   go into `--arm-mass`, `--load-mass` and `--arm-length` (kg, m).
2. **Wire.** Supply off. Servo cable to the adapter's servo port; the adapter's power input to the bench supply; the
   adapter's USB to the PC. Set the supply to 12.0 V and its **current limit to 3 A** (the script also stops above
   3 A; the servo's stall current is not known yet, so raise the limit only if the seller's listing gives a higher
   one) before switching it on. Check that the servo's ID is 1 (or pass `--id`) and
   that no other servo is on the bus.
3. **Check the registers first.** `uv run python scripts/servo_bench.py run --port PORT --schedule short --load-mass 0
   --arm-mass 0 --arm-length 0.15 --out check.csv` with no arm fitted: it prints the gains, and the log must show the
   position following the goals, the voltage near 12 V and a temperature near room temperature. If the values make no
   sense, the register addresses in `erg/sts.py` differ from this servo's; stop and report it in #87.
4. **Set the zero.** Fit the arm so that it hangs straight down at about the servo's middle position (2048 steps); if
   it is off by more than a few degrees, pass the hanging position's steps with `--zero`.
5. **Run.** Keep hands clear of the arm's swing (±1.2 rad, about ±70°). For each load (none, the arm only, and the
   arm with the load): `uv run python scripts/servo_bench.py run --port PORT --schedule full ... --out
   load-NAME.csv`. Let the servo cool to below 40 °C between runs. The supply's current display should stay below
   the limit; if the supply limits the current, the run is not valid (note it).
6. **Fit.** `uv run python scripts/servo_bench.py fit load-*.csv --out fit.json`.

Attach to #87: the CSV logs, `fit.json`, the printed gains, the rig's measured masses and length, the supply's
voltage and current limit, the adapter's model, the room temperature, and a photo of the rig. Do not attach serial
numbers, device addresses or anything that identifies the PC or its network.
